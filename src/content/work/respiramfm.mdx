## The problem

Audio-only models for respiratory disease detection hit a ceiling. A wheeze sounds like a
wheeze whether the patient is a 70-year-old with diagnosed COPD or a 6-year-old with a
seasonal infection, and the sound alone throws away the clinical context that a physician
would use immediately. RespiraMFM's premise is that if you pair the audio with the patient's
history and symptoms as text, and align the two modalities contrastively, the model learns
representations that transfer much better — especially to diseases it was never fine-tuned on.

That premise depends entirely on there being text to align against. There wasn't, at least
not in usable form.

## What I actually worked on

Public respiratory audio datasets are collected by different groups, for different studies,
over roughly two decades. They do not share a schema. The same clinical concept appears as a
free-text note in one dataset, a coded categorical in another, and is simply absent from a
third. Age might be an integer, a bucketed range, or a string like `"adult"`. Recording
location on the chest is encoded with per-dataset abbreviations that collide across datasets
— the same two-letter code means different anatomical sites depending on where it came from.

My contribution was the pipeline that extracts, normalises, and converts that heterogeneous
patient metadata across all seven datasets into standardised natural-language inputs suitable
for multimodal training. Concretely that meant three things:

**Extraction.** Getting the metadata out at all. It lives in per-dataset formats — spreadsheets,
sidecar text files, filename conventions that encode subject and channel — and each needed its
own reader.

**Normalisation.** Mapping every dataset's vocabulary onto one shared set of fields, and being
explicit about missingness rather than silently imputing. A field that is genuinely unknown
has to stay unknown in the text, because the alternative is teaching the model that every
patient from one cohort has the same fabricated attribute.

**Verbalisation.** Turning normalised records into fluent natural-language descriptions the
text encoder can use. This is the step that is easy to get subtly wrong. Templates that are
too rigid make every record from a given dataset share long verbatim substrings, which hands
the contrastive objective a shortcut: it can match audio to text by recognising the dataset's
template rather than the clinical content. Getting the phrasing varied enough to avoid that,
while staying faithful to the underlying values, was most of the work.

## Result

RespiraMFM was evaluated across five major respiratory diseases using all seven datasets, in
both supervised fine-tuning and zero-shot settings. It improved AUROC by 9.15% on supervised
tasks and 20.98% on zero-shot tasks over existing baselines. The paper was accepted to the
**ACL 2026 Main Conference**.

The size of the zero-shot gain relative to the supervised one is the part I find most
interesting, and it is indirectly a statement about the text pipeline. Zero-shot transfer to
an unseen disease can only work if the textual side generalises — if the model has learned
something about the relationship between clinical language and sound, rather than memorising
seven dataset-specific dialects.

## SkillEvolBench: building tasks designed to break agents

The second paper from this lab took me in a different direction. **SkillEvolBench** asks
whether an LLM agent's accumulated episodic trajectories can actually be distilled into
reusable procedural skills, or whether "skill libraries" are mostly an illusion. It contains
180 tasks across six real-world agent environments, organised into role-conditioned families
that share latent procedures. Agents learn on acquisition tasks, write to an external skill
library, and are then evaluated on frozen deployment tasks.

I constructed benchmark tasks by generating procedural workflows and then systematically
perturbing them, along four axes designed to defeat shallow reuse:

- **Information gaps** — a step the stored skill assumed would be available is missing.
- **Context shifts** — the same underlying procedure, presented in a different surface context.
- **Adversarial shortcuts** — a plausible-looking path that produces a wrong answer, to catch
  agents pattern-matching instead of reasoning.
- **Failure conditions** — states where the correct behaviour is to recognise the procedure
  does not apply.

The point of the perturbations is separation. A benchmark where the deployment tasks resemble
the acquisition tasks cannot distinguish an agent that formed an abstraction from one that
memorised a trace. The perturbation axes are what force that distinction to show up in the
numbers.

## What the benchmark found, including the uncomfortable part

Across ten model configurations and three agent harnesses, the finding was largely negative:
current agents adapt locally but rarely form robust, reusable skills. Gains from skill-based
conditions were unstable under frozen deployment.

The result I think is most worth stating plainly is that **raw-trajectory reuse frequently
outperformed distilled skills.** Handing the agent the unprocessed episode beat handing it the
tidy extracted "skill." The distillation step was discarding contextual and procedural detail
that turned out to still be useful. Capacity analysis pointed the same way: writing more skills
or maintaining larger resource libraries improved coverage while introducing episode-specific
drift and procedural clutter.

That is not the result anyone building a skill-library system hopes for, and it is the reason
the benchmark is worth having.

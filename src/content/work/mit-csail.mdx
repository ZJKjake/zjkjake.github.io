## Why the infrastructure is the hard part

Humanoid reinforcement learning has a measurement problem that is easy to underestimate until
you have been burned by it. Runs are long, expensive, and high-variance. Two training runs of
the *same* configuration with different random seeds can land far enough apart that the gap
between them exceeds the gap you are trying to attribute to a method change. If you compare a
single run of method A against a single run of method B, the honest interpretation of the
result is usually "no information."

Layer on top of that: the two candidate methods rarely have the same parameter count unless
someone deliberately makes them, GPU jobs die partway through, and the environments themselves
— InterMimic and SONIC inside NVIDIA Isaac Lab — each have their own configuration surface and
their own assumptions about reference motion data. Every one of those is an opportunity to
produce a number that looks like a result and is not one.

Benchmark infrastructure is the thing that closes those holes. It is not glamorous and it is
the difference between a project that can make claims and one that cannot.

## What I built

**Checkpointing and evaluation.** Long humanoid runs fail. The infrastructure has to make a
failure at hour six recoverable rather than fatal, and it has to keep evaluation separable from
training so a policy can be re-evaluated later, under a different protocol, without retraining
it. That separation is what makes it possible to change your mind about how to measure
something after the fact.

**Multi-seed aggregation.** Running several seeds per configuration and aggregating them, so
what gets reported is a distribution rather than whichever run happened to finish first. This
is the single highest-leverage piece. It is also the one that costs the most compute, which is
why it is the first thing dropped under deadline pressure and the reason it needs to be
automatic rather than a decision someone makes per experiment.

**Parameter matching.** Enforcing that architectures under comparison are matched in capacity
before their scores are put side by side. Without it, "method A beats method B" can quietly
mean "A had more parameters than B." I had already learned this one the hard way during
[an earlier internship](/work/agile-robots), where several of my own architecture comparisons
were not parameter-matched and I had to caveat them rather than trust them. Building the check
into the harness means nobody has to remember to do it.

**Reference-motion validation.** Motion-imitation environments consume reference motion data,
and malformed or misaligned references produce training runs that fail in ways that look like
algorithmic failure. Validating references up front converts a confusing multi-hour failure
into an immediate, legible error.

**GPU resource management and cost accounting.** Scheduling across available GPUs, plus
automated analysis of performance, memory, and cost per run. The cost part matters more than it
sounds: knowing what a sweep will cost before launching it is what makes it possible to choose
between "more seeds" and "more configurations" on evidence instead of vibes.

## Scaling

I scaled the harness from 256 to 1,024 parallel environments — a fourfold increase in
throughput per wall-clock hour, which converts directly into either more seeds or more
configurations at the same cost. The requirement was that reproducibility survive the change:
scaling the environment count must not silently alter batch composition or evaluation
semantics, or every comparison against previously collected results is invalidated.

## What I take from it

The pattern I keep running into is that the reliability of a research result is set by
infrastructure decisions made before any of the interesting work starts. Whether comparisons
are parameter-matched, whether multiple seeds are the default or an afterthought, whether
evaluation can be re-run independently of training — those are all decided by the harness, and
by the time someone is looking at a plot it is too late to change the answer.

That is the part of machine learning I want to keep working on.
